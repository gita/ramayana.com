## The poem Maharshi Valmiki gave us

A child hears a beloved story from a mother, then asks to hear it again. Affection makes room for attention. We can approach the Valmiki Ramayana with that same willingness to listen.

The Sanskrit epic is called the Adi Kavya [the first poem], and Maharshi Valmiki the Adi Kavi [the first poet]. Tradition counts about 24,000 shlokas [verses]. Devotees place its events in the Treta Yuga.

Maharshi Valmiki is said to have uttered the first shloka in grief when a hunter killed one of a pair of krauncha birds beside the Tamasa river. Lava and Kusha, the sons of Shri Ram and Mata Sita, learned the poem from him and sang it. This scripture invites listening.

## Finding your place in the kandas and sargas

The Valmiki Ramayana has seven kandas [books], divided into sargas [chapters]. Commonly printed texts contain around 500 sargas. The books appear in this order:

1. Bala
2. Ayodhya
3. Aranya
4. Kishkindha
5. Sundara
6. Yuddha, also called Lanka
7. Uttara

When choosing an online reader, look for clear book and chapter navigation. You should be able to return to the passage you were reading without searching through a long page. On a phone, that small convenience helps you keep a daily reading appointment.

## What a good online edition should offer

Look for the original Sanskrit beside a faithful translation. Word meanings help you pause over an unfamiliar expression and see how the translation relates to the original. Clear verse references make passages easier to find again.

The edition should also be named, with the translator or commentary identified wherever that information is available. These details let you know whose explanation you are reading. A translation helps us understand, while access to the Sanskrit keeps the original before us.

### Are the Valmiki Ramayana and Ramcharitmanas the same book?

They tell the story of Shri Ram in distinct works. Maharshi Valmiki composed the Sanskrit epic. Goswami Tulsidas retold the story in Awadhi in the Ramcharitmanas during the 16th century. You can read them alongside one another, giving each text attention in its own language and form.
